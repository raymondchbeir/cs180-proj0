Becoming Friends
with Your Camera

An exploration of how camera position and focal length change perspective, facial proportions, architectural depth, and the apparent relationship between foreground and background.

Raymond Chbeir•CS180: Intro to Computer Vision and Computational Photography
View project ↓

Project idea

Perspective depends on where the camera is.

In each experiment, I changed camera distance and focal length while trying to preserve the size of the main subject in the image. Moving the camera changes perspective because it changes the relative distances from the camera to different points in the scene. Zooming changes the field of view, letting us restore similar framing from a new camera position.

Part 1

Selfie: The Wrong Way vs. The Right Way

01

I photographed the same person from three distances. As I moved farther away, I increased zoom so the face stayed roughly comparable in size. The closest portrait shows the strongest perspective distortion; the farther portraits look increasingly natural.

Why does the farther portrait look different?

When the camera is very close, the nose is much closer to the camera than the ears and sides of the face. That relative depth difference exaggerates nearby features. After stepping back, those depth differences are small compared with the total camera distance, so facial proportions look flatter and more natural. Zoom restores the subject's size in the frame; it is the changed camera position that changes the perspective.

Part 2

Architectural Perspective Compression

02

I first photographed the building from farther away with a tighter field of view, then moved closer and used a wider field of view. The change in camera position changes the apparent depth and convergence of the architecture.

Why does distance create compression?

From farther away, the camera-to-object distances for the near and far parts of the building are relatively similar, so their projected sizes differ less. The scene therefore appears flatter. From closer up, the difference between those distances is a larger fraction of the total distance, so nearby surfaces appear larger and lines converge more strongly, emphasizing depth.

Part 3

The Dolly Zoom

03

For the dolly zoom, I used the stone sphere as the foreground subject and the Campanile/building area as the background. Across ten sequential frames I changed my camera position while compensating with zoom, creating the characteristic shift in the background relative to the foreground.

10-frame animated dolly zoom

IMG_1271 through IMG_1280, animated in the same order they were photographed.

What creates the effect?

A dolly zoom couples two changes: camera translation changes perspective, while a compensating change in focal length keeps the foreground subject at approximately the desired scale. Because objects at other depths do not transform in exactly the same way, the background appears to expand or contract relative to the subject. This is the classic “Vertigo shot” effect.
